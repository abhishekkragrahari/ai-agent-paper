# Semantic Byzantine Faults: A New Fault Model for LLM-Agent Distributed Systems

**Position Paper — Draft v0.1**

## Abstract

Classical distributed systems theory characterizes failures along a well-understood spectrum: fail-stop (crash) faults, at one end, in which a process simply stops; and Byzantine faults, at the other, in which a process may deviate from its protocol in arbitrary — including adversarial — ways. Four decades of consensus, replication, and Byzantine fault tolerance (BFT) research have built quorum-based guarantees around this dichotomy, from the 3f+1 bound of Lamport, Shostak, and Pease to practical systems such as PBFT. Today, a new class of distributed system is emerging in which the "processes" are autonomous large language model (LLM) agents that communicate, delegate tasks, and jointly maintain shared state. We argue that these systems exhibit a failure mode that the classical dichotomy does not capture: an agent can follow the *communication protocol* exactly — sending well-formed messages, at the right times, to the right recipients, passing every liveness and syntactic-integrity check a distributed system would apply — while silently corrupting the *meaning* carried by those messages, through hallucination, instruction drift, or context staleness. We call this a **semantic Byzantine fault (SBF)**: a fault that is invisible to protocol-level fault detectors and is *not* covered by standard BFT quorum guarantees, because those guarantees bound the number of protocol-arbitrary nodes, not the number of protocol-compliant-but-meaning-corrupt nodes. We formalize the distinction, show by construction that standard BFT quorum intersection arguments fail to provide safety under SBFs, survey how recent empirical findings on multi-agent LLM failures are in fact undocumented instances of this fault class, and lay out a research agenda — semantic quorum certificates, meaning-level state machine replication, and drift-aware view changes — for building LLM-agent systems with the same rigor the field has historically demanded of distributed systems.

## 1. Introduction

Distributed systems research has always been organized around a taxonomy of faults, because the taxonomy determines what guarantees are even achievable. A system that assumes only crash faults can use relatively cheap majority-quorum protocols (e.g., Paxos, Raft) to tolerate f failures among 2f+1 nodes. A system that must tolerate nodes behaving arbitrarily — including maliciously — needs the much more expensive machinery of Byzantine fault tolerance, and even then only up to f faulty nodes among 3f+1 [Lamport, Shostak & Pease 1982; Castro & Liskov, PBFT]. The entire edifice of consensus theory rests on a clean definition: a faulty node is one whose *outputs* can be arbitrary, and correctness proofs reason over the set of possible output sequences a faulty node could produce.

A new and rapidly growing class of system breaks an assumption this taxonomy has always quietly relied on: that a node's output is either syntactically checkable against protocol, or (in the Byzantine case) *unboundedly* arbitrary and therefore treated with maximal suspicion. LLM-agent systems — pipelines of autonomous agents that plan, call tools, pass messages, and jointly build and consume shared state — occupy an uncomfortable middle ground. An LLM agent almost always emits protocol-conformant output: a well-formed JSON message, a correctly addressed reply, an on-time response. What it may get wrong is the *content*: it may hallucinate a fact, misremember an earlier instruction, act on a stale or drifted view of shared context, or misinterpret another agent's intent while remaining perfectly well-behaved at the protocol layer.

Recent empirical work confirms that this is not a hypothetical concern. Large-scale studies of multi-agent LLM frameworks report failure rates between 41% and 87% in production-style traces, and attribute the large majority of these failures — on the order of 79% — to specification ambiguity, inter-agent miscommunication, and state desynchronization rather than to base-model incapability [see §6]. Concurrently, hallucination research has begun reframing hallucination itself as a *drift* phenomenon: a gradual, layer-by-layer divergence of an agent's internal representation from ground truth, and in multi-agent settings, a divergence between *concurrent* agents' internal states — what one recent paper terms "context drift" [see §6]. These are, in our view, independent rediscoveries of the same underlying systems phenomenon, described so far only empirically and only within individual system architectures. What is missing is a fault model: a formal vocabulary, borrowed from and compatible with classical distributed systems theory, in which this phenomenon can be named, bounded, and reasoned about — so that the guarantees we build for LLM-agent systems can be stated as precisely as the guarantees we build for crash- and Byzantine-fault systems.

This paper proposes that vocabulary. Our contributions:

1. We define the **semantic Byzantine fault (SBF)**, a fault class in which a node is *protocol-faithful but meaning-unfaithful*, and locate it formally between crash faults and classical Byzantine faults.
2. We show, by direct construction, that standard BFT quorum-intersection safety arguments — which guarantee correctness whenever any two quorums of size 2f+1 (out of 3f+1) overlap in at least one correct node — do **not** transfer to SBFs, because a quorum built entirely of protocol-faithful nodes can still unanimously certify a semantically corrupted value.
3. We argue that current mitigations in the LLM-agent literature (multi-agent voting, "council" consensus, context-synchronization protocols) are, in our framework, informal and partial instances of two well-known distributed-systems techniques — quorum certification and state machine replication — applied without the semantic-layer guarantees those techniques normally require, and we explain precisely what is missing.
4. We lay out a concrete research agenda: semantic quorum certificates, meaning-aware view-change protocols, and a drift metric that can serve as the semantic-layer analogue of a checksum.

This is a position paper. We do not present a full protocol or an implementation; our goal is to establish the fault model and show, with the formal rigor the systems community is used to, why it is necessary — a necessary precursor to the protocol work in items 3 and 4.

## 2. Background: The Classical Fault Taxonomy

We briefly recall the two poles of the classical spectrum, since our contribution is defined relative to them.

**Crash (fail-stop) faults.** A faulty process halts and produces no further output. Systems tolerating only crash faults can achieve consensus with 2f+1 nodes to tolerate f faults (e.g., Paxos, Raft), because a correct process can always distinguish "silence" from "a message."

**Byzantine faults.** A faulty process may exhibit arbitrary behavior, including sending different, contradictory messages to different recipients, or colluding with other faulty processes. The seminal result of Lamport, Shostak, and Pease established that reliable agreement requires at least 3f+1 processes to tolerate f Byzantine faults in the general (oral-message, no-signature) case, later reduced in message complexity by practical systems such as PBFT. The crucial assumption underwriting *every* BFT protocol is that the set of faulty nodes is bounded by f and that correct nodes are not merely rule-following but **semantically reliable**: a correct node that receives well-formed input X produces the *appropriate* output f(X). BFT proofs never need to distinguish "correct but wrong" from "correct," because under the classical model there is no such category — a non-Byzantine node is assumed to compute f(X) faithfully, full stop.

This last point is the assumption our fault model breaks.

## 3. The Semantic Byzantine Fault

**Definition.** A node exhibits a *semantic Byzantine fault* in round r if (a) its message m_r is well-formed and protocol-conformant — it would pass any syntactic, timing, authentication, or liveness check the protocol specifies — and (b) the *content* of m_r is not a faithful function of the node's true observed inputs, due to internal error (hallucination, context staleness, instruction drift, misremembered state) rather than protocol violation or adversarial intent.

The defining property of an SBF is condition (a): it is **protocol-invisible**. Every mechanism distributed systems normally use to detect a misbehaving node — malformed messages, missed heartbeats, signature failures, equivocation detectable by comparing two messages bit-for-bit — is silent in the presence of an SBF, because the fault lives entirely in a layer the protocol does not inspect: the mapping from the node's true context to the content it reports.

This places SBFs in a specific, previously under-named region of the fault spectrum:

- They are **not crash faults** — the node is live and responsive.
- They are **not classical Byzantine faults** in the operational sense the term is used for protocol design, because a Byzantine-fault-tolerant protocol's detection and voting machinery is built entirely around *protocol-layer* signals (message well-formedness, equivocation, timing), none of which an SBF necessarily violates. (We note that the *original*, most general definition of "Byzantine" — arbitrary behavior — technically subsumes SBFs as a special case. Our point is that the entire *practical apparatus* built on top of that definition — quorum certificates, equivocation detection, signature-based accountability — implicitly assumes faulty behavior is protocol-detectable in the limit, which an SBF is not. We use "semantic Byzantine" precisely to flag that it is Byzantine in the classical *definitional* sense but escapes every *operational* BFT mechanism.)
- They are **not adversarial** in the game-theoretic sense BFT is designed against, though nothing in the definition precludes an adversary from deliberately inducing them (e.g., prompt injection designed to trigger drift) — which makes them, if anything, a strictly harder target to defend, since they are a superset encompassing both benign and adversarially-triggered instances, all sharing the same protocol-invisibility.

## 4. Why Quorum Safety Fails Under SBFs

Classical BFT safety rests on **quorum intersection**: any two quorums of size 2f+1 drawn from n ≥ 3f+1 nodes share at least f+1 nodes, hence at least one correct node, which prevents two conflicting values from both being certified. The proof that this guarantees safety depends critically on the assumption we identified in §2: a correct (non-Byzantine) node that is part of two different quorums reports the *same*, *true* value both times, because correctness implies semantic fidelity, not just protocol fidelity.

Consider a set of LLM agents, none of which is Byzantine in the protocol-detectable sense — none equivocates, none forges signatures, none is offline — but a subset independently hallucinates the same plausible-but-false value (a real and empirically observed phenomenon: LLMs given similar prompts and similar drifted context frequently converge on the same *wrong* answer, because the drift is driven by shared biases in training and shared staleness in context, not by independent random noise). A quorum of 2f+1 such nodes — all "correct" by every protocol-level check — can unanimously, non-equivocally certify a semantically false value. No quorum-intersection argument catches this, because quorum intersection guards against *disagreement* among quorums, not against *correlated error within* a quorum. Standard BFT is explicitly built to tolerate an adversary who can make faulty nodes lie in a coordinated way — but it assumes the *number* of such nodes is bounded by f and, critically, that non-faulty nodes are semantically independent oracles for the truth. SBFs violate that independence assumption directly: the very thing that makes LLM agents useful — shared training, shared context, similar prompting — is exactly what makes their errors *correlated* rather than independent, which is the worst possible failure structure for a voting-based quorum system.

This is, we believe, the precise formal reason why naive "multi-agent voting" or "council" approaches to reducing LLM hallucination provide much weaker guarantees than their designers often implicitly claim by analogy to BFT: the analogy to Byzantine quorum systems only holds if agent errors are adversarially bounded in count *and* statistically independent given correctness, and SBFs satisfy neither.

## 5. Implications and a Research Agenda

We do not claim to solve the SBF problem here; we claim it needs to be named and tackled with systems-level rigor rather than purely empirical patching. We outline three directions.

**5.1 Semantic quorum certificates.** A quorum certificate in classical BFT certifies that 2f+1 nodes *agreed*. A semantic quorum certificate would need to additionally certify *diversity of derivation* — that the agreeing nodes reached their answer via sufficiently independent evidence paths (different context windows, different tool calls, different upstream sources), so that agreement is informative about truth rather than merely informative about shared bias. This requires a measurable notion of "independence" for LLM reasoning traces, which does not yet exist in a form usable as a protocol primitive.

**5.2 Drift-aware view changes.** Classical BFT view-change protocols are triggered by timeout — the suspicion that the current leader has crashed or is equivocating. A drift-aware analogue would need a trigger condition sensitive to the *rate of semantic divergence* between an agent's reported state and reconstructable ground truth (e.g., via periodic grounding checks against an authoritative source), treating sustained high drift as fault evidence even in the complete absence of protocol violations.

**5.3 A semantic checksum.** Networked systems detect corruption with checksums computed over bits. We conjecture that an analogous *meaning-level* integrity check is possible — not a cryptographic hash, but a cheap, structural comparison (e.g., entailment or consistency scoring between an agent's output and its cited context) that a receiving agent or a monitor process can apply before trusting a message, analogous to how a network stack drops a packet that fails its checksum before it ever reaches the application layer. The open research question is whether such a check can be made cheap and reliable enough to run at the message rate real agent systems operate at, and what false-positive/false-negative tradeoff it implies for overall system liveness.

We deliberately leave the *mechanisms* underspecified here: the contribution of this paper is the fault model and the demonstration that it is not reducible to existing categories, which we believe is the necessary first step before mechanism design can be evaluated against a clear correctness target.

## 6. Related Work

Our proposal sits at the intersection of two literatures that have so far developed largely independently.

*Classical fault tolerance.* The crash/Byzantine dichotomy and its quorum-based guarantees trace to Lamport, Shostak, and Pease's Byzantine Generals result and are made practical in systems such as PBFT (Castro & Liskov). This literature is mature but, by construction, silent on faults below the protocol layer.

*Empirical multi-agent LLM failure analysis.* Large-scale trace studies of production multi-agent frameworks report failure taxonomies dominated by specification and coordination issues rather than base-model limitations, clustering failures into system-design issues, inter-agent misalignment, and verification gaps. This work is the empirical evidence base our fault model seeks to formalize; it documents SBF-class failures without naming them as a distinct fault class or connecting them to the quorum-safety argument in §4.

*Hallucination-as-drift.* Recent work reframes hallucination as a gradual internal representational drift, and — closest to our own framing — proposes "context drift" as the mechanism by which concurrent agents' internal states diverge, motivating *synchronization protocols* as a mitigation. We view this as an important, complementary empirical characterization of *why* SBFs occur; our contribution is orthogonal, showing *what formal guarantees fail* once they occur, independent of the specific drift mechanism.

*Multi-agent consensus as hallucination mitigation.* Proposals to dispatch queries to multiple heterogeneous LLMs and synthesize via a consensus model are, in our terms, an informal, partial instance of a semantic quorum certificate (§5.1): using heterogeneity across model families as a (weak, unmeasured) proxy for the independence condition we argue is necessary for a quorum argument to transfer to the semantic layer. Our framework suggests a principled question this line of work does not yet answer: how much cross-model heterogeneity is *enough*, and can that threshold be derived rather than assumed, analogous to the 3f+1 bound in classical BFT?

*Fundamental limits on hallucination control.* Separately, a recent impossibility result argues, from mechanism-design and scoring-rule theory, that no LLM capable of non-trivial knowledge aggregation can simultaneously guarantee truthful representation, information conservation, and knowledge-constrained optimality. This result is complementary evidence for our position: if individual-agent semantic faithfulness cannot be guaranteed by construction, then system-level fault tolerance — rather than single-model correctness — is the only remaining lever, which is precisely the systems-theoretic reframing this paper argues for.

## 7. Limitations and Scope

This is a position paper and we are explicit about what it does not do. We do not present an implemented protocol, a simulation, or empirical measurement of SBF rates in a real deployed system; §5's mechanisms are conjectures about a feasible research direction, not evaluated designs. We also do not formally prove an impossibility result of the kind BFT theory provides (e.g., an analogue of the 3f+1 bound for SBFs); §4 is a constructive counterexample showing existing guarantees fail, not a derivation of what guarantee, if any, is achievable and at what cost. We consider deriving that bound — the "3f+1 of semantic fault tolerance," if it exists — the most important open technical question raised by this paper, and outside the scope of a position paper to answer.

## 8. Conclusion

Distributed systems research has always advanced by naming its failure modes precisely enough to build provable guarantees against them. LLM-agent systems are now failing in production at rates the crash/Byzantine taxonomy does not explain, because that taxonomy — and every quorum-based guarantee built on top of it — implicitly assumes that a node's protocol conformance and its semantic fidelity are the same property. We have argued they are not, named the gap the semantic Byzantine fault, shown constructively that it breaks the central safety argument of BFT quorum systems, and reframed several existing empirical mitigations as partial, informal instances of classical systems techniques applied without the guarantees those techniques normally require. We believe the next step for this line of work is mechanism design against this explicit fault model — starting with the open question of whether a bound analogous to 3f+1 exists for semantic fault tolerance, and if so, at what cost in message complexity and agent diversity.
